What I keep hearing: the AI pain

You've seen the headlines. AI isn't hype anymore. It's settling into the economy, and you want to figure out how to get into it before the window narrows. So you've read the articles, watched the YouTube explainers, maybe taken a course or two. And somehow you're no closer to being in than when you started. The field that was an infant two years ago is already a teenager. Chasing this exponential speed feels exhausting, and the chase doesn't quite add up to a position you'd actually want to be in.

What real AI-era work asks of you

Real AI-era work isn't about knowing the AI keywords that get you surfaced by algorithms, or using AI tools, or shipping AI features in your product. AI is a general-purpose technology, on the level of electricity or the internet. A shift that big doesn't just require new tools. It requires reinventing how you think, work, and make decisions.

When you use AI without grounding in human science, what you actually have is a system predicting the next likely word. It feels like progress. Yet, two costs often go unnoticed here. One is what gets built: fast-food AI. Beautiful junk that feels like work but doesn't compound. The other is what gets lost: your own cognitive instincts that made you good at the work in the first place and can’t be engineered (so far).

How I help you: AI that compounds what only you can do

When you put the prediction engine next to your human mind, the cognitive load redistributes. AI handles the cross-subject pattern-scanning that no single lifetime could cover by hand. Your bandwidth opens up for non-AI-able work*: developing original ideas, applying the fingerprint expertise you've accumulated through thousands of small, atypical decisions you've made daily, for years. Here's what that looks like in practice:

  • A PM who can't code now is the AI PM who ships working prototypes that convey product vision before burning dev hours. Those coded prototypes double as behavioral specs for engineering, faster than any PRD.
  • A founder building an AI business now finds the niche only they can occupy in their industry. That focus compounds into a new market category, with customers who came specifically for what only they could build.
  • A designer who wasn't sure what to do with their legal background now combines the two with AI in the mix. The hybrid that once felt like a wandering path now compounds into the kind of work companies pay premium for and others can't quite copy.
